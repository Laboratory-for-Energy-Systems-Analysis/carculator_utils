FCEV electrical power and buffer plausibility

14 October 2026. This is a diagnostic follow-up to the earlier FCEV accounting correction. Fresh runs cover all eight installed 2020 FCEV car sizes and seven FCEV truck sizes, Swiss climate, WLTC for cars and Regional delivery for trucks. The physical specifications agree with the installed Premise parameter companions.

Finding: mechanical and electrical power are mixed

The previous correction closes the declared nominal allocation, but that is not an electrical feasibility test. power and electric power are mechanical motor-shaft ratings in the driving-cycle calculation. The fuel-cell rating divided by its own-consumption multiplier is net electrical output. Adding battery electrical power to this output must cover motor conversion losses and auxiliary electricity. Currently the sum merely equals shaft power.

With the existing 2020 motor/inverter efficiency of 0.9, the appropriate boundary is:

required DC kW = motor shaft kW / 0.9 + auxiliary DC kW
net fuel-cell kW = gross stack kW / gross-to-net multiplier
required buffer kW = max(required DC kW - net fuel-cell kW, 0)

The 0.9 input already describes motor/inverter conversion; no separate inverter loss is added. Stack own consumption is included once. Auxiliaries bypass the motor. The current Swiss-climate auxiliary demand is used here, not an extreme temperature design envelope. Battery internal losses enter stored-energy calculations, not this terminal-output sum.

All fifteen templates have this boundary mismatch. Preserving the existing fuel-cell/battery shares on the electrical side is the proposed correction; retaining fuel-cell ratings and increasing only the buffer is an alternative. The user has been asked to choose the allocation. Neither has been installed. Both require consistent source-model mass feedback; neither requires a solver in Premise. The existing share parameters do not establish which design choice was intended.

Cycle screening does not certify the batteries

The audit independently recovers DC demand from wheel energy, transmission and motor efficiencies and checks it against the hydrogen-input trace multiplied by fuel-cell efficiency. Every cell passes this energy identity.

All car WLTC traces remain below their net fuel-cell rating. Under an instantaneously responding fuel cell they therefore need no battery discharge assistance. This does not validate acceleration at rated motor output, startup, fuel-cell ramping or regenerative charge acceptance.

The 40 t truck needs up to 150.29 kW of battery assistance on Regional delivery, while its current trace assumes regenerative DC recovery reaching 261.62 kW. These are different charge and discharge requirements. The model has no validated FCEV-specific battery charge-power limit or SOC controller.

The 3.5 t and 7.5 t trucks exceed their motor shaft ratings for ten seconds each, with peaks of 53.87 versus 51.06 kW and 107.01 versus 93.74 kW, respectively. Correcting the electrical allocation alone does not resolve these mechanical shortfalls. The 32 t truck exceeds its recorded buffer requirement for three seconds even though its motor rating is sufficient. These counts describe the selected traces, not measured road-test performance.

An optimistic periodic energy calculation gives minimum usable storage of 1.71--6.42 kWh for trucks, below their 32.72--88.56 kWh nominal capacities. It allows instantaneous full fuel-cell power, unrestricted charging and discharging, freely chosen initial SOC and curtailment. This establishes only a lower bound. FCEV battery DoD defaults are zero, so no operating SOC window is available to test usable capacity. The lower-bound controller is not used to revise hydrogen consumption or replacement counts.

Battery specification evidence

Car buffers combine 0.24 kWh/kg cell energy density, 2.1304 kW/kg cell power density and a 70% cell mass fraction. Their implied pack energy density is 168 Wh/kg; their declared output corresponds to 1.491 kW/kg of complete pack. The power source lists several references together and supplies no common duration, SOC or temperature for the paired energy/power assumptions. The Medium car consequently has 3.93 kWh in 23.38 kg. This is not a validated dedicated FCEV buffer specification.

For comparison, Toyota's second-generation Mirai announcement of 30 November 2020 gives 310.8 V, 4 Ah, 44.6 kg and 31.5 kW for ten seconds. Nominal energy calculated from voltage and charge is 1.2432 kWh, or 27.9 Wh/kg of pack. This is a late-2020 launch reference, not an all-class fleet average or a continuous-power rating.

Hyundai's 2020 Nexo emergency guide, printed page 9 gives a lithium-ion-polymer pack of 1.56 kWh and 51.2 kg, or 30.5 Wh/kg. Its 2018 launch specification reports 40 kW from the battery, 95 kW from the fuel cell and a 120 kW motor. The battery power duration is unspecified. Market variants differ: the Australian emergency guide lists a 113 kW motor, so these two motor ratings are not merged into one calibration.

These references support reviewing the car pack's joint mass/energy/power calibration. Their different chemistry and architecture prevent declaring the generic model pack physically impossible solely from the comparison.

The truck's inherited 0.15 kW/kg parameter explicitly describes a lead-acid starter. It cannot validate the final lithium-ion traction buffer, which is instead energy-sized at 20 kWh plus 6% of hydrogen chemical energy. The 40 t pack is 71.10 kWh and 423.22 kg. Hyundai's retrospective on the Swiss XCIENT fleet introduced in 2020 reports 72 kWh total across three batteries, a 180 kW fuel-cell system and a 350 kW motor. Its similar capacity supports only an order-of-magnitude comparison: it does not validate the model's pack mass, power limits or smaller fuel-cell rating. No matched continuous/pulse power source has been established for the model's NMC-622 pack.

Implementation status and reproducibility

dev/audit_premise_fcev_power.py produces power_screen.csv, the complete model snapshot, extracted parameter sources and a run manifest with package paths, source hashes and installed-parameter hashes. Nine focused unit tests pass for electrical/mechanical boundaries, repeated-cycle storage, charge and discharge losses, regeneration and invalid inputs. Full-catalogue trace and installed-template comparisons pass in the recorded Python 3.12 environment.

No model default, vehicle workbook, Premise runtime or LCIA score changes in this diagnostic step. The current future-year policy retains FCEV hardware; the range-preserving battery-resizing contract is restricted to BEV/PHEV. IAM hydrogen-efficiency scaling does not repair these baseline power ratings. The full legacy-versus-new EF 3.1 comparison remains a later step.

Next actions, one issue at a time: settle the electrical allocation and correct it with native-model regressions; address the two undersized truck motors; then agree paired buffer specifications and explicit charge/discharge limits. The unrelated PHEV cycling review remains skipped as requested.
